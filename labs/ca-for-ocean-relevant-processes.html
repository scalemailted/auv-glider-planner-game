1. Why use CA for ocean-relevant process analogs?

CA and grid-process methods are useful when a class is learning how local state, neighborhood influence, and time stepping create moving environmental patterns. They can honestly represent event/process layers: where a simplified bloom is active, where a plume edge might be, where recovery is happening, or where revisit value is increasing.

CA is a rigorous first step in computational modeling, not a shortcut for fake hotspots.

The best sample is not always the brightest cell. The best sample is often the one that most improves the mission's belief about a moving, uncertain ocean feature under flow, depth, energy, risk, and communication constraints.

2. What CA can model honestly

CA / grid-process models are useful for teaching local update rules and generating simplified event/process layers.

Honest uses

Local activation, fronts, patch growth, recovery, source cells, thresholds, domain rules, and freshness/revisit timing.

Mission value

They create inspectable scenarios where students can reason about sampling a moving or changing field.

3. What CA cannot model by itself

Simple CA is not a calibrated hydrodynamic ocean simulator. CA alone does not produce final AUV adaptive-sampling priority.

More science-aligned environmental models use advection, diffusion, source/sink, decay/growth, flow coupling, hidden truth, forecast, belief, uncertainty, observations, data assimilation, and acquisition logic.
Mission-planning models use sampling priority, route cost, flow assist/opposition, hazard/risk, depth profile, energy, surfacing windows, redundancy, and regret.

4. Bloom growth / decay

Bloom Growth / Decay

What it models: a simplified biological or optical event layer with patch expansion and weakening.

Scientific inspiration: local growth, mortality, nutrients, and changing visibility.

CA/grid mechanism: active cells grow into nearby susceptible cells; older cells decay or recover.

State variables: background, susceptible, active bloom, decaying, recovered.

Local update idea: cells near active bloom cells become active; active cells eventually decay.

What the colors mean: bright cells are active bloom; dim cells are decaying or low intensity.

What it teaches: intensity, edge growth, and revisit timing.

Requires flow coupling? yes, for physical transport or deformation.

Requires uncertainty? yes, for realistic mission decisions.

Mission-planning relevance: sample edges, fronts, uncertain growth zones, and stale regions.

Sampling strategy: sample boundary and uncertainty, not only the brightest center.

What this is not: not a calibrated ecology, chlorophyll, or harmful algal bloom forecast.

Future higher-fidelity version: reaction-advection-diffusion with nutrients, light, mortality, sensor noise, and acquisition value.

What to notice: patch center intensity and sampling priority can diverge.

5. River plume front

River Plume Front

What it models: a coastal source creates a spreading freshwater or contaminant boundary.

Scientific inspiration: river discharge, salinity contrast, coastal transport, and mixing.

CA/grid mechanism: source cells + propagating front + diffusive spread.

State variables: ocean background, source, plume interior, plume edge, dissipating trail.

Local update idea: cells near active plume cells become plume-edge cells; edge cells can become interior plume cells or dissipate.

What the colors mean: bright source/interior, medium uncertain edge, dim dissipating trail.

What it teaches: source, boundary, spread, and why edge uncertainty matters.

Requires flow coupling? yes - physical downstream transport belongs in the Flow Fields and Coupled Sampling Space labs.

Requires uncertainty? yes, because the useful target is often the uncertain boundary.

Mission-planning relevance: validate plume position and boundary displacement.

Sampling strategy: sample the uncertain boundary, not only the plume center.

What this is not: not a calibrated hydrodynamic or salinity model.

Future higher-fidelity version: advection-diffusion-source-decay model with flow field F(x,y,t), sensor noise, forecast uncertainty, and acquisition value.

Plume / Front Analog Widget

Step a source-fed plume analog and watch the edge move.

What to notice: the uncertain edge can be more mission-relevant than the intense source.

6. Oil / chemical plume

Oil / Chemical Plume

What it models: a contaminant event layer with source, spread, dilution, and decay.

Scientific inspiration: pollutant release, chemical weathering, and transport.

CA/grid mechanism: source injection, local spread, decay, and thresholded concern zones.

State variables: clean, contaminated edge, contaminated interior, decaying, restricted.

Local update idea: contamination expands to neighbors, decays over time, and may create hazard penalties.

What the colors mean: brighter means more contaminant intensity, not automatically higher priority.

What it teaches: source/sink thinking and hazard-aware sampling.

Requires flow coupling? yes.

Requires uncertainty? yes.

Mission-planning relevance: balance validation, safety, and reachability.

Sampling strategy: sample safe boundary transects and forecast-error regions.

What this is not: not a fate-and-transport model.

Future higher-fidelity version: flow-coupled advection-diffusion-decay with hazard masks and sensor limits.

7. Marine plastic / drifting pollutant

Marine Plastic / Drifting Pollutant

What it models: a drifting accumulation or debris-probability layer.

Scientific inspiration: floating debris, convergence zones, and retention regions.

CA/grid mechanism: local accumulation, leakage, and directed neighborhood bias.

State variables: clear, low debris, high debris, trapped, dispersing.

Local update idea: nearby debris increases local debris likelihood; trapped zones hold activity longer.

What the colors mean: debris likelihood or event intensity.

What it teaches: accumulation and retention differ from simple spread.

Requires flow coupling? yes.

Requires uncertainty? yes.

Mission-planning relevance: validate convergence and locate uncertain accumulation bands.

Sampling strategy: sample convergence edges and forecast disagreement zones.

What this is not: not a calibrated particle-tracking model.

Future higher-fidelity version: Lagrangian particle or concentration model with currents, windage, uncertainty, and observation updates.

8. Turbidity event / density current

Turbidity Event / Density Current

What it models: a simplified turbid water event or downslope density-driven pulse.

Scientific inspiration: sediment resuspension, runoff, and density-driven flow.

CA/grid mechanism: threshold release, directed propagation, and decay.

State variables: clear, loaded, turbid, settling, recovered.

Local update idea: loaded cells activate near a trigger and spread along a preferred direction.

What the colors mean: turbidity intensity and recent settling.

What it teaches: threshold events and trailing recovery.

Requires flow coupling? yes.

Requires uncertainty? yes.

Mission-planning relevance: track leading edge, avoid unsafe zones, and revisit recovery areas.

Sampling strategy: sample leading edge and settling gradient.

What this is not: not a sediment transport or density-current solver.

Future higher-fidelity version: bathymetry-aware transport with settling velocity, flow, and sensor noise.

9. Thermocline / water-mass boundary

Thermocline / Water-Mass Boundary

What it models: a simplified boundary between water masses or depth-layer properties.

Scientific inspiration: fronts, stratification, and gradients.

CA/grid mechanism: boundary cells shift, thicken, or weaken based on neighbors.

State variables: water mass A, boundary, water mass B, mixed.

Local update idea: boundary cells move where neighboring states disagree.

What the colors mean: region identity and boundary uncertainty.

What it teaches: gradients can matter more than extrema.

Requires flow coupling? yes.

Requires uncertainty? yes.

Mission-planning relevance: boundary tracking and validation transects.

Sampling strategy: cross the boundary, not only sample one side.

What this is not: not a 3D stratified ocean model.

Future higher-fidelity version: depth-aware thermodynamic model with observations, forecast uncertainty, and glider profile constraints.

10. Eddy-trapped patch

Eddy-Trapped Patch

What it models: a patch retained inside a rotating or recirculating feature.

Scientific inspiration: mesoscale/submesoscale eddies and retention zones.

CA/grid mechanism: local persistence and circular neighborhood bias.

State variables: background, trapped patch, edge, leaking trail.

Local update idea: patch cells persist inside a retention mask and leak at edges.

What the colors mean: retained intensity and uncertain boundary.

What it teaches: persistence, recirculation, and boundary monitoring.

Requires flow coupling? yes.

Requires uncertainty? yes.

Mission-planning relevance: map retention boundary and detect leakage.

Sampling strategy: sample around the ring and suspected escape pathways.

What this is not: not a vortex-resolving ocean simulation.

Future higher-fidelity version: flow-driven tracer model with eddy velocity field and ensemble forecast spread.

11. Shoreline runoff pulse

Shoreline Runoff Pulse

What it models: a short source pulse entering from shore.

Scientific inspiration: stormwater, nutrients, sediments, and coastal discharge.

CA/grid mechanism: boundary source cells activate, spread, then decay.

State variables: clean, source pulse, active runoff, decaying, recovered.

Local update idea: shoreline cells seed activity that moves into nearby water cells.

What the colors mean: pulse strength and decayed trail.

What it teaches: event timing and source-driven uncertainty.

Requires flow coupling? yes.

Requires uncertainty? yes.

Mission-planning relevance: intercept event plume while respecting coastal hazards.

Sampling strategy: sample cross-shore transects and forecast leading edge.

What this is not: not a watershed or nearshore circulation model.

Future higher-fidelity version: source-time-series plus coastal transport, bathymetry, and sensor observations.

12. Hypoxia / recovery zone

Hypoxia / Recovery Zone

What it models: a low-oxygen event layer and later recovery.

Scientific inspiration: oxygen depletion, respiration, mixing, and re-aeration.

CA/grid mechanism: depletion spreads locally; recovery occurs after mixing or time.

State variables: normal, stressed, hypoxic, recovering.

Local update idea: stressed cells become hypoxic near hypoxic neighbors and recover slowly.

What the colors mean: depletion intensity and recovery state.

What it teaches: risk, recovery, and revisit planning.

Requires flow coupling? yes.

Requires uncertainty? yes.

Mission-planning relevance: monitor risky zones and recovery boundaries.

Sampling strategy: sample boundary gradients and areas with stale oxygen belief.

What this is not: not a calibrated biogeochemical model.

Future higher-fidelity version: oxygen budget model with mixing, respiration, forecast uncertainty, and sensor profiles.

13. Persistent monitoring / freshness field

Persistent Monitoring / Freshness Field

What it models: how value returns when an area has not been sampled recently.

Scientific inspiration: age of information and persistent monitoring.

CA/grid mechanism: sampled cells reset; unsampled cells age and regain value.

State variables: fresh, aging, stale, overdue.

Local update idea: time increases staleness; sampling locally resets staleness.

What the colors mean: higher color means older belief or higher revisit value.

What it teaches: low event intensity can still have high sampling priority.

Requires flow coupling? sometimes, if revisit need depends on transported features.

Requires uncertainty? yes, because stale belief increases uncertainty.

Mission-planning relevance: allocate routes across revisit windows.

Sampling strategy: revisit stale areas while avoiding redundant repeated samples.

What this is not: not a full belief covariance model.

Future higher-fidelity version: age-of-information plus forecast uncertainty and route-aware revisit planning.

What to notice: stale cells can become high priority even when event intensity is low.

14. How flow coupling changes the model

Flow turns a local event layer into a transported environmental process. Without flow coupling, a plume front is a teaching analog. With flow coupling, the model can begin to ask where currents carry the event and which routes are helped or opposed by the field.

C_next = C + advection_by_F + diffusion + source - decay
In words: the concentration changes because currents move it, diffusion spreads it, sources add it, and decay removes it.

15. How uncertainty changes the sampling priority

Uncertainty separates the hidden truth from the forecast and belief. A sample is useful when it reduces the right uncertainty or validates the right forecast error for the mission.

belief_next = update(belief_current, observation, model)
In words: the mission uses a sample to change what it believes about the hidden process.

16. Event intensity is not sampling priority

Event intensity says how much phenomenon is present.

Sampling priority says how useful it is to sample there now.

  • A bloom center may have high intensity but low priority if already well understood.
  • A plume boundary may have lower intensity but high priority if uncertain.
  • A stale monitoring region may have high priority because the model has not been updated recently.
  • A high-value cell may have low priority if it is unreachable, risky, redundant, or costly.
sampling_priority = expected_value + uncertainty + boundary_value + hidden_event_suspicion + forecast_validation_value + staleness - travel_cost - current_risk - hazard_penalty - redundancy_penalty
In words: priority combines event value and information value, then subtracts route cost, current risk, hazards, and redundant sampling.

17. From CA analogs to more true environmental models

From CA analog to mission-grade sampling model

CA event layer: x_i(t+1) = f(x_i(t), N_i(t), theta)
In words: each cell updates from its state, neighborhood, and parameters.
Deterministic environmental process: C_next = C + diffusion + source - decay
In words: the field spreads, receives input, and weakens over time.
Flow-coupled process: C_next = C + advection_by_F + diffusion + source - decay
In words: currents move the feature while other process terms change it.
Stochastic truth/forecast process: hidden truth evolves, forecast is imperfect, samples are noisy
In words: the mission acts on a belief, not on perfect truth.
Belief update: observations update belief mean and uncertainty
In words: each sample should improve what the model knows.
Sampling priority: priority = value + uncertainty + boundary value + hidden-event suspicion - travel cost - risk - redundancy
In words: a good sample is useful, reachable, safe, and not redundant.

18. How ANCHOR uses these as mission scenarios

ANCHOR uses CA and grid-process analogs as transparent teaching scenarios. Later engine contracts should separate static world, hidden truth, expected/forecast state, observations, belief, uncertainty, hidden-event probability, and visible planning state.

These analogs prepare students for the engine ladder without pretending that the current CA pages implement full ocean physics, data assimilation, or production route planning.
