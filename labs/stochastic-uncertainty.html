1. Why uncertainty?

The first three labs assumed the world was known. In real sampling problems, the vehicle usually has a forecast, uncertainty around that forecast, noisy observations, and beliefs that change over time.

Perfect knowledge asks: What is truly valuable?
Uncertainty asks: What do we believe is valuable, and how confident are we?
Hidden truth T(x,y,t)
Forecast / prior E(x,y,t) or μ_prior(x,y,t)
Observations z_i
Posterior belief μ_post(x,y,t)

Connection to the simulator: the Uncertainty / Forecast Demo shows forecast, truth, uncertainty, information gain, forecast error, and delta after update.

2. Deterministic vs stochastic worlds

A deterministic process gives the same result from the same initial state and rules. A stochastic process includes random variables or uncertain outcomes. Random-looking seeded examples can still be deterministic if the seed is fixed.

X(t+1) = F(X(t))
In words: the deterministic model gives one next state.
X(t+1) ~ P(X(t+1) | X(t))
In words: the stochastic model gives a distribution over possible next states.
What to notice: stochastic models do not say anything can happen. They define how likely different outcomes are.

3. Random variables, distributions, and realizations

A random variable represents an uncertain quantity. A distribution says how likely possible values are. A realization is one sampled outcome.

X ~ P(X)
In words: X is a random variable drawn from a probability distribution.
x_sample ~ P(X)
In words: a realization is one possible outcome from the distribution.

Distribution and Realization Widget

Sample Bernoulli, Normal / Gaussian, or Bimodal outcomes and watch the histogram form.

What to notice: one realization can be misleading. A distribution describes many possible realizations.

4. Hidden truth, expected state, observed state, and belief state

This is the core uncertainty stack: hidden true state, expected or forecast state, observed samples, and updated belief state.

T(x,y,t)
In words: what is actually happening.
E(x,y,t) or μ_prior(x,y,t)
In words: what the model predicted before new observations.
z_i = T(x_i,y_i,t_i) + ε_i
In words: a measurement is truth at the sampled location plus sensor noise.
μ_post(x,y,t)
In words: what the model believes after using observations.

Hidden Truth / Forecast / Observation Widget

Add sparse noisy observations, adjust forecast bias and sensor noise, and optionally reveal the hidden truth.

What to notice: the vehicle does not observe the whole truth. It updates belief from sparse noisy samples.

5. Inaccurate forecasts vs hidden unknowns

Not all surprises mean the same thing. A forecast error means we were wrong about something we thought we knew. A hidden unknown means we discovered something we did not know to look for.

Inaccurate expected state

The expected layer exists, but its value, location, timing, strength, or shape is wrong.

Examples: front shifted, hotspot weaker, current underestimated.

Planning response: correct forecast, update uncertainty, reduce confidence, replan.

Hidden unknown state

A real phenomenon was not represented in the forecast at all.

Examples: unexpected plume, hidden bloom layer, chemical anomaly.

Planning response: create a hypothesis, gather confirmatory samples, reweight objectives.

Short version: inaccurate expected state means the forecast was wrong about something it already represented. hidden unknown means samples suggest a new phenomenon. hidden-event confidence should increase only when surprise is coherent, persistent, and context-consistent.

innovation = observed_value - expected_value
In words: innovation is the gap between what was measured and what the forecast expected at that point.
surprise = abs(innovation) / sqrt(expected_uncertainty^2 + sensor_noise^2)
In words: surprise measures how unusual the observation is compared with expected uncertainty and sensor noise.
hidden_event_evidence = surprise + spatial_coherence + persistence + signature_match + context_consistency
In words: one surprising sample is not enough to confirm a hidden event.

Forecast Error vs Hidden Event Widget

Compare shifted fronts, weakened hotspots, hidden anomalies, and noisy false alarms.

What to notice: forecast error changes an existing layer. Hidden unknowns create or activate a new hypothesis layer.

6. Observations and sensor noise

Measurements are not perfect. Sampling at one point tells you something local, but not everything about the field.

z_i = T_i + &epsilon_i
In words: observed value equals truth plus measurement error.
&epsilon_i ~ Normal(0, σ_sensor^2)
In words: sensor noise is modeled as random error with zero mean and sensor variance.
What to notice: repeated measurements can reduce noise uncertainty, but they cost time and coverage.

7. Bayesian updating

Bayesian updating combines prior belief and new evidence. The prior is what we believed before observing; the likelihood says how compatible the observation is with each possible state; the posterior is what we believe after observing.

p(T | z) proportional_to p(z | T) p(T)
In words: posterior belief is proportional to how likely the observation is under a possible truth times how much we believed that truth before.

Bayesian Cell Update Widget

Adjust prior event probability and sensor reliability, then compare detected vs not detected observations.

What to notice: a detection does not always mean the event is real. It depends on the prior and sensor reliability.

8. Markovian state evolution

A Markov model assumes the next state depends on the current state, not the entire past.

P(X_{t+1} | X_t, X_{t-1}, ...) = P(X_{t+1} | X_t)
In words: the current state summarizes what matters for predicting the next step.

Markov Transition Widget

Step a probability distribution over Clear, Anomaly, and Recovering states.

What to notice: Markovian does not mean deterministic. It means current state controls future probabilities.

9. Hidden Markov intuition

Sometimes the important state is hidden. The vehicle observes signals that provide evidence about the hidden state.

H_t evolves by a Markov transition
In words: the real phenomenon changes secretly over time.
Z_t is generated from H_t with sensor noise
In words: measurements give clues about the hidden state but do not reveal it perfectly.
What to notice: the belief can be wrong even when the update is mathematically reasonable.

10. Gaussian fields: GP and GMRF intuition

A Gaussian Process is a smooth field model where nearby points are correlated by a kernel. A Gaussian Markov Random Field is a spatial belief model where local neighbors carry most of the dependency.

f(x) ~ GP(m(x), k(x,x'))
In words: a Gaussian process describes a distribution over possible smooth fields.
p(X_i | all other cells) = p(X_i | neighbors)
In words: a GMRF says each cell mainly depends on nearby cells.

Gaussian Field Intuition Widget

Use a lightweight kernel smoother to see how samples influence mean and uncertainty.

What to notice: samples reduce uncertainty nearby. This is GP-style educational interpolation, not a full GP solver.

11. Forecasting, ground truth, and data assimilation

A forecast is an expected state before observations. Ground truth is what actually happened. Data assimilation updates the forecast using observations.

forecast error = truth - forecast
In words: forecast error is known only when truth is revealed.
innovation = observation - forecast_at_observation
In words: innovation is what we can compute during the mission from observations and forecast.
Connection to the simulator: truth is shown in the Uncertainty / Forecast Demo for education. Fair planning should treat truth as hidden unless oracle mode is explicit.

12. Measuring uncertainty, confidence, surprise, and calibration

Uncertainty / variance

σ^2(x,y,t)

How spread out possible values are.

Entropy

H(p) = -sum p_i log(p_i)

How uncertain a probability distribution is.

Surprise

abs(z - μ) / sqrt(σ^2 + σ_sensor^2)

How unexpected an observation is.

Calibration

When the model says 80% confidence, it should be right about 80% of the time.

Confidence is trust in an estimate or hypothesis, not certainty.

13. Regret and value of information

Regret measures what was lost by acting on imperfect belief. A sample is useful if it helps choose better actions later.

regret = oracle value - belief-based value
In words: regret is the opportunity missed because the planner did not know the truth.
sampling_priority = expected_value + information_gain + forecast_validation_value + hidden_event_discovery_value - cost
In words: sample priority can include value, learning, validation, discovery, and travel cost.

Regret and Information Value Widget

Choose a candidate sample site, reveal the truth, and compare against the oracle best choice.

What to notice: the best-looking expected value is not always the best learning action.

14. Acquisition value and next-best sampling

An acquisition function scores where to sample next. It can combine high expected value, high uncertainty, expected information gain, forecast validation, hidden-event confirmation, front classification, and revisit value.

A(x,y,t) = w_value μ(x,y,t) + w_uncertainty U(x,y,t) + w_surprise H_event(x,y,t) - w_cost cost(x,y)
In words: the acquisition map is a weighted score that turns belief and uncertainty into sampling priority.

Acquisition Map Widget

Adjust weights for expected value, uncertainty, hidden-event suspicion, and cost.

What to notice: changing the mission objective changes the sampling map, even if the belief stays the same.

15. How this connects to stochastic coupled sampling

The deterministic coupled lab used S*(x,y,t) = h(V,F,C,M). The stochastic coupled lab will use A(x,y,t) = h(μ,U,F,C,M).

S*(x,y,t) = h(V,F,C,M)
In words: oracle objective under perfect knowledge.
A(x,y,t) = h(μ,U,F,C,M)
In words: belief-based objective under uncertainty, flow, constraints, and mission context.
